##THE WORLD WE PRETEND TO LIVE IN...
___

When did we learn to watch a city burn and wait to find out which side we are supposed to be on before deciding how to feel?

It did not happen overnight. It was taught slowly. Breaking news. Panel debates. Carefully arranged outrage. Grief that had to be sorted before it was allowed to land. We were trained to hold our humanity in suspension until affiliation arrived to authorize it.

A city is bombed. Somewhere, a phone lights up with a final message that will never be answered. Somewhere else, a child learns to tell the difference between the sound of drones and the sound of planes. But before any of that is allowed to mean anything, we ask: who started it?
Not because we care about truth first.
Because we have learned not to feel without permission.

Every war arrives with a justification. And the justification always sounds like the last available option.

The language is careful. It has a job to do. To make a decision taken in a closed room feel like it was made by history itself. Like the war simply happened. Like no one chose it.
The moral case for war is not discovered.
It is produced.

We are told there are rules.

Sovereignty. Territorial integrity. International law.
Simple ideas. Necessary ones.
No state may violate another. That is the promise.
And yet, the map of modern history tells a different story. The same principle that is invoked to condemn some actions is quietly set aside to excuse others. Not because the rule is unclear, but because power decides when it applies.

The principle is universal.
Its protection never was.

There is a theory that does not pretend otherwise.
Realism does not lie to you. It says power is the only currency that matters. That morality is performance. That the weak suffer because the system allows them to be made to suffer.
Watch how wars actually begin, and it becomes difficult to dismiss.

Wars do not begin when injustice demands them.
They begin when someone calculates they can win.

But there is something realism cannot answer.
If there are no rules, only power, then every state that breaks them today is writing the script for how it may be broken tomorrow.
After the Second World War, the world said never again.
It built institutions. Wrote charters. Created courts.
And then ensured that the most powerful would never truly be subject to any of it.
The system was not designed to fail.
It was designed to function selectively.
It demands compliance from the weak and negotiates exceptions for the strong. It names violations, but punishes them unevenly. It holds meetings, passes statements, and calls it order.
This is not collapse.
This is design.
For that design to survive, something else is required.
Forgetting.
Not accidental forgetting. Structured forgetting.
The kind that improves the story over time. That sands down the edges. That turns decisions into inevitabilities and consequences into footnotes.
Violence does not sustain itself on force alone.
It survives because memory is managed.
And so we learn not to flinch.
A defence minister announces the most intense bombing campaign in a generation with the calm of someone reading a weather report. And the world absorbs it. Processes it. Moves on.
That composure is not maturity.
It is damage.
Behind every framework, every theory, every legal argument, there is a question none of them can fully escape:
What do we owe each other?
Not as citizens.
Not as allies.
As people.
There was once an attempt to answer that honestly. To say that suffering anywhere is not a distant problem. That a life lost in a place you have never seen still alters the world you live in. That solidarity is not sentiment, but survival.
Because the alternative is simple.
A world where force decides everything.
And nothing else is allowed to matter.
War is a decision.
Not an accident. Not fate. Not history unfolding on its own.
A decision.
Which means it has authors.
Which means it had alternatives.
Which means something else was possible, and someone chose otherwise.
The most political thing an ordinary person can do now is refuse the forgetting.
Not the official version. The complete one. The one that includes uncertainty, cost, and the choices that were made when other choices existed.
Because this world we pretend to live in—this clean, justified, orderly version of events—does not exist.
It is maintained.
And it only holds as long as we agree not to look too closely.
